01 · Our work on the river

Six ways we bring a chalk stream back to life

Chalk streams are rare: fewer than 300 exist worldwide, and nearly all of them are in England. Each of these jobs answers a specific problem we have measured on the ground.

  • Gravel riffle restoration

    We tip washed flint gravel into straightened reaches so trout can spawn again and the water runs clean over stone rather than silt.

  • Volunteer planting days

    Every second Saturday from October to March, groups of up to 30 plant sedges, water crowfoot and alder on eroded banks. Tools, gloves and tea are provided.

  • Invasive species removal

    Himalayan balsam and New Zealand pygmyweed are cleared upstream before they seed. Each pulled patch is logged on the Sallow Brook map.

  • Bank stabilisation with willow

    Coir rolls and live willow spiling hold soft banks in place while roots take hold, cutting the fine silt that smothers spawning gravel.

  • Water-quality monitoring

    212 citizen scientists test phosphate, nitrate and oxygen every month at sites we train them on. Every reading goes into the public record, including the bad ones.

  • School and company days

    Pupils and corporate teams spend a day in the river with an ecologist, from kick-sampling to planting, and go home with a report for their school or office.

02 · The money trail

How your gift works

  1. 1

    Your gift is tied to a named reach

    Donations go into a restoration fund with a river reach attached. A one-off £72 gift pays for 4 m² of spawning gravel and a season of balsam clearance on Reach 3.

  2. 2

    Ecologists plan, volunteers do the work

    Our ecologists survey each reach first. Contractors move heavy stone, volunteers plant and clear, and every task is booked against the gift that paid for it.

  3. 3

    You see the result, with the numbers

    Within 30 days you get a photo log and water readings for your reach. Each spring we publish a statement showing that 91p of every pound spent on rivers went into the water.
